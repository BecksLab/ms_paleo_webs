  \begin{abstract}
    Ecological networks are increasingly used to compare community
    organisation, quantify interaction turnover, and infer responses to
    environmental change. However, interaction data are often
    incomplete, requiring researchers to construct networks using
    different approaches that encode their own ecological assumptions.
    The extent to which these choices influence macroecological
    inference remains under explored. We investigate this problem using
    four successive marine assemblages spanning the Early Toarcian
    Extinction Event and subsequent recovery. We compare six food-web
    models representing feasibility-based, realised, and structural
    approaches to network construction. By quantifying network topology,
    pairwise interaction turnover, and extinction dynamics, we assess
    how model choice influences inferences about ecological change
    through time. Model choice explained substantially more variation in
    network topology than temporal turnover across extinction and
    recovery phases. Models also predicted markedly different sets of
    pairwise interactions, even when their overall network structures
    were similar. Although species-level vulnerability rankings were
    comparatively robust across models, link-dependent extinction
    outcomes were more model-dependent, indicating that conclusions
    about ecological dynamics can depend on how interactions are
    reconstructed. Our findings demonstrate that ecological networks are
    not neutral representations of communities, but model-dependent
    representations that can shape macroecological inference. Network
    reconstruction should therefore be treated as part of hypothesis
    specification rather than a technical step preceding analysis.
    Matching models to inferential goals and testing conclusions across
    alternative network representations are essential for distinguishing
    robust ecological patterns from those contingent on modelling
    assumptions.
          \par\bigskip
      \noindent\textbf{Keywords:} Network
reconstruction, Macroecology, Ecological networks, Interaction
turnover, Trophic organisation, paleoecology, Ecosystem resilience
      \end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

Understanding how biological communities are organised and how species
interact is a central goal of ecology. While early efforts focused
primarily on species richness and composition, there is growing
recognition that communities are structured not only by which species
occur, but also by how they interact (Eisenhauer et al., 2026; Gravel et
al., 2019; Mestre \& Bastazini, 2026; Thuiller et al., 2024).
Interaction networks are increasingly used as macroecological state
variables to compare community organisation across environmental
gradients (Tylianakis \& Morris, 2017), quantify \(\beta\)-diversity in
interaction structure (Poisot et al., 2015; Trøjelsgaard \& Olesen,
2016), evaluate stability--complexity relationships (Allesina \& Tang,
2012), and assess vulnerability to global change (Hao et al., 2025).
Consequently, ecological networks have become central to comparative
analyses of communities under future environmental change (Hao et al.,
2025; Michalska-Smith \& Allesina, 2019) as well as understanding
environmental transitions across deep time (Dunhill et al., 2024;
Roopnarine, 2006).

However, ecological networks are rarely fully observed. Interaction data
are often incomplete, and sampling effort varies across contemporary,
biogeographic, and historical contexts (Jordano, 2016; Poisot et al.,
2021; Sandra et al., 2025). Interactions and network structure must
therefore frequently be inferred through predictive approaches rather
than reconstructed directly from observations (Strydom et al., 2021). A
key, often implicit, assumption is that the variation in these
reconstructed network properties reflects ecological differences among
communities rather than methodological artefacts introduced during the
construction process (Brimacombe et al., 2023; Fründ et al., 2016).

Interactions are commonly inferred indirectly from traits (\emph{e.g.,}
body size), phylogenetic relationships, species co-occurrence, or
theoretical constraints (Morales-Castilla et al., 2015; Strydom et al.,
2021). Network construction is therefore not simply an observational
exercise, but an inference step shaped by assumptions about how
interactions arise. Different network prediction frameworks encode
different assumptions, representing interactions as either biologically
feasible combinations of traits, realised diets shaped by energetic
optimisation, or networks constrained by structural properties (Gauzens
et al., 2025; Guimarães, 2020; Petchey et al., 2011; Strydom et al.,
2026). These assumptions act as `structural priors' that can shape
network architecture, potentially generating different representations
of trophic structure, even for the same species pool. If alternative
approaches systematically produce different network configurations,
comparisons among communities risk conflating ecological variation with
differences imposed by the prediction method. The reliability of
macroecological inference therefore depends not only on the available
data, but also on the structural assumptions underlying network
construction.

Despite rapid methodological development in interaction prediction, and
comparisons among methods within individual frameworks (\emph{e.g.,}
Allesina et al. (2008) and Pichler et al. (2019)), the extent to which
alternative network-construction approaches alter macroecological
conclusions remains insufficiently explored. This is consequential for
comparative studies, in which network metrics are routinely interpreted
as indicators of community properties, dynamics, and differences
(Allesina \& Tang, 2012; Delmas et al., 2018; Poisot et al., 2015). When
alternative approaches impose different constraints on interaction
topology, apparent differences among communities may partly reflect the
assumptions of the framework used for prediction rather than underlying
ecological processes. Establishing the sensitivity of macroecological
conclusions to these assumptions is therefore essential for interpreting
patterns inferred from predicted networks.

Deep-time ecosystems provide a useful context in which to examine this
problem. Their trophic interactions cannot be observed directly and must
instead be inferred from traits, co-occurrence, and theoretical
constraints (Dunhill et al., 2024; Dunne et al., 2008; Dunne et al.,
2014; Roopnarine, 2006; Smith et al., 2025). Network-based inferences
about these systems are consequently dependent on prediction, making the
assumptions underlying alternative approaches both unavoidable and
potentially consequential. Here, we use the Early Toarcian Extinction
Event (ETEE; \textasciitilde183 Ma) as a case study. Recent work by
Dunhill et al. (2024) used reconstructed food webs to infer changes in
community stability and structure during this event, concluding that the
extinction selectively affected specialist and infaunal organisms,
producing simplified ecosystems increasingly dominated by ecological
generalists and opportunistic taxa. Rather than treating these
conclusions as independent of the reconstruction method, we ask a
broader question -- to what extent are ecological inferences about the
ETEE contingent on the model used to reconstruct trophic interactions?

To address this question, we reconstruct ecological networks for four
successive assemblages spanning the ETEE using six contrasting
prediction approaches that represent feasibility-based, realised, and
structural approaches to network construction. By holding species
composition constant while varying the prediction method, we isolate the
influence of model choice on inferred network structure and the
ecological conclusions derived from it. We assess whether conclusions
drawn from a single framework, including those of Dunhill et al. (2024),
remain robust across alternative, equally plausible representations of
interaction structure, or whether different approaches yield
substantially different interpretations of ecological change. More
broadly, this case study examines how strongly macroecological
conclusions derived from ecological networks depend on the assumptions
embedded in the approaches used to construct them.

\section{Methods}\label{methods}

\subsection{Study system and fossil
data}\label{study-system-and-fossil-data}

We used fossil occurrence data from the Cleveland Basin spanning the
late Pliensbachian to the late Toarcian, encompassing the ETEE and
subsequent ecological recovery. To characterise changes in network
structure across this interval, we defined four successive
paleo-assemblages: pre-extinction (Pliensbachian), post-extinction
(early Toarcian), early recovery (early--middle Toarcian), and late
recovery (late Toarcian). For each taxon, we recorded body size and
characterised ecological traits using Bambach's ecospace framework
(Bambach et al., 2007), coding tiering, motility, and feeding mode
following Dunhill et al. (2024). Each paleo-assemblage was treated as a
community of potentially interacting taxa. The dataset comprised 57 taxa
representing diverse marine groups, including cephalopods, bivalves, and
gastropods. Further details on the dataset, including taxonomic
composition, trait coding, and temporal resolution, are provided in
Dunhill et al. (2024).

\subsection{Network construction
models}\label{network-construction-models}

\subsubsection{Conceptual classification of network
types}\label{conceptual-classification-of-network-types}

In this study, \emph{model} is used as an umbrella term for frameworks
that encode assumptions about trophic interactions and generate
ecological networks from specified inputs. Although these approaches
differ in theoretical foundations and in their mechanistic, statistical,
or structural character, this operational definition enables consistent
comparison of the ecological hypotheses they represent.

We classify network reconstruction models into three conceptual classes:
feasibility-based, realised, and structural models, each representing
distinct hypotheses about how trophic interactions arise and constrain
food-web structure (Strydom et al., 2026).

\textbf{Feasibility-based models} identify biologically possible
consumer--resource interactions using trait compatibility or mechanistic
rules, defining a feasible interaction space without necessarily
predicting which interactions occur in a specific community. In
paleoecological contexts, they translate trait information and expert
knowledge into biologically grounded predictions when direct interaction
observations are unavailable (\emph{e.g.,} Fricke et al. (2022);
Roopnarine (2006); Shaw et al. (2024)).

\textbf{Realised models} estimate the subset of feasible interactions
expected under ecological constraints, incorporating factors such as
energetic optimisation, mechanical limitations, or probabilistic trait
relationships to predict putatively realised diets (Brose et al., 2006;
Schneider et al., 2016). Unlike feasibility-based models, they
explicitly represent assumptions about resource selection and the
processes shaping interactions.

\textbf{Structural models} generate networks using general topological
rules governing properties such as species richness, connectance, or
trophic organisation, rather than species-specific ecological
information (Allesina et al., 2008). They therefore do not reconstruct
community-specific interactions but provide theoretical reference
networks analogous to null models.

We selected six models to sample distinct assumptions rather than
exhaustively compare available methods (Table~\ref{tbl-models}): one
feasibility-based model (PFWIM), three realised models (ADBM, ATN,
Body-size ratio), and two structural models (Random, based on the
Erdős--Rényi framework, and Niche). Together, they represent alternative
approaches to translating species information or general structural
constraints into network predictions.

interactions. Thus, network differences may reflect both input data
(\emph{e.g.} functional traits vs body size) and the ecological
assumptions and structural constraints encoded by each model. We
selected PFWIM because it formalises biologically possible
consumer--resource relationships in a framework applicable across
datasets, offering a transferable alternative to system-specific expert
assignment of links, although other approaches exist (\emph{e.g.,}
Roopnarine (2006)).

This classification enables us to assess how assumptions about trophic
interactions influence network structure and ecological interpretation.
By holding species composition constant while varying models, we assess
whether differences in network properties and macroecological
conclusions reflect model assumptions rather than species composition.

\subsubsection{Network generation and
replication}\label{network-generation-and-replication}

We evaluated six network reconstruction models using the approaches
listed in Table~\ref{tbl-models}, with expanded descriptions of model
assumptions, parameterisation, and link-generation rules are provided in
Supplementary Material S1, including full mathematical formulations and
parameter definitions. For each community, 100 stochastic network
realisations were generated per model (n = 2400 in total, 600 per time
bin). Of the simulated networks we excluded those that had a network
metric that was considered an outlier (\(> 3\sigma\) from the mean).
This resulted in the removal of 13 networks (of which 3 were ATN model
networks and the rest Random model networks), typically due to very
large trophic level values. COntinuous body sizes were assigned from the
categorical size classes. This was done by sampling from a truncated
lognormal distribution, with the upper and lower bounds of each size
class serving as the boundaries. Alternative body size distributions
(lognormal and uniform) were tested and shown to have a minimal impact
on topology (Supplementary Material S1; Figure S1). Random and Niche
models were parameterised using connectance values drawn from an
empirically realistic range (0.05--0.25; Curtsdotter et al. (2011)). For
the Random model, which requires link number explicitly, connectance
(Co) was first converted to expected link number (L) using
\(L = Co\times S^2\), ensuring consistency in network density across all
models. Richness (S) was determined by the number of taxa for each time
bin. For the Body-size ratio model, we followed the approach of Yeakel
et al. (2014) using only the body-mass scaling to determine links
between species. Sensitivity of network structure to these parameter
choices is evaluated in Supplementary Material S1. The PFWIM generates a
deterministic metaweb for a given trait configuration. To allow for
statistical analyses we introduced variation by applying a downsampling
procedure that randomly pruned links until the connectance of the
network decreased by 0.01. This step allowed us to introduce statistical
variation while still preserving most of the original metaweb structure.
The choice of the magnitude of connectance change and its effects on
network structure are discussed in Supplementary Material S1.

\subsection{Network metrics and structural
analyses}\label{network-metrics-and-structural-analyses}

We quantified network structure using a suite of network metrics
(Table~\ref{tbl-properties}), capturing overall network properties,
motif structure, and species-level variability. Differences among models
were assessed using a multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA), with
model identity as a fixed factor and the full set of network metrics as
response variables. Variance partitioning was further assessed using
permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA). Pairwise
interaction turnover was quantified using link-based \(\beta\)-diversity
and was calculated following the framework of Poisot et al. (2012),
among four of the models (ADBM, ATN, body-size ratio, and PFWIM). Random
and Niche models were excluded because they are species-agnostic models
that generate networks matching broad topological properties
(\emph{e.g.,} connectance and degree distributions) rather than
predicting biologically realistic species-specific interactions.
Specifically, we looked at interaction rewiring among shared species
(\(\beta_{OS}\), see S1 for additional details on the decomposition of
interaction \(\beta\)-diversity and its implementation), which allows
separation of differences arising from altered interaction identities
among species common to both networks. Because all networks within a
given assemblage are constructed from the same species pool, differences
in interaction structure primarily reflect `rewiring' of trophic links
due to stochasticity in the model rather than species turnover. All
calculations were performed for all model combinations within the same
assemblage (time bin).

\subsection{Extinction simulations and their
evaluation}\label{extinction-simulations-and-their-evaluation}

Following Dunhill et al. (2024), we simulated species loss from
pre-extinction networks under trait-based, network-position--based, and
random removal scenarios. Species were deleted sequentially, with
cascading secondary extinctions allowed to propagate. Simulated
post-extinction states were compared to observed (\emph{i.e.,}
reconstructed from fossil occurrence data) networks using mean absolute
differences (MAD) of food web metrics (Table~\ref{tbl-properties}) and
modified true skill statistics (TSS) calculated separately at the node
level (species presence/absence) and link level (presence/absence of
interactions between species pairs). Scenarios were ranked within each
model based on MAD and TSS performance, and Kendall's rank correlation
coefficient (\(\tau\)) was used to quantify agreement in scenario
ordering across models. Detailed implementation of extinction sequences
and secondary extinction rules is provided in Supplementary Material S1.

\subsection{Software and
Reproducibility}\label{software-and-reproducibility}

Ecological network reconstruction and extraction of structural metrics
were conducted in Julia v1.12.7 (Bezanson et al., 2017) using the model
implementations provided in Supplementary Material S1. All statistical
analyses, including MANOVA, PERMANOVA, and post hoc comparisons, were
performed in R v4.5.2 (R Core Team, 2024). The full analytical workflow,
including data preprocessing, network generation, extinction
simulations, and metric calculation, is fully reproducible from the
archived codebase.

\section{Results}\label{results}

\subsection{Network structure differs among
models}\label{network-structure-differs-among-models}

Across modelling approach, network structure (network properties listed
in Table~\ref{tbl-properties}) differed significantly (MANOVA, Pillai's
trace = 2.79, approximate \(F_{30,11880}\) = 502.1, \(p<\) 0.001),
indicating that the model choice systematically alters inferred food web
topology. Linear discriminant analysis (LDA) identified two dominant
axes of variation, explaining 95.2\% of the differences between models.
LDA axis one correlated with trophic index, vulnerability, and
generality. LDA axis two correlated with competition index, generality,
and connectance (Panel A Figure~\ref{fig-structure}; Table S1, Figure
S1). Broadly indicating that structural differences are driven by the
way in which the models assign links to species as opposed to
macro-level network properties such as maximum trophic level.

\subsubsection{Variance partitioning of network
structure}\label{variance-partitioning-of-network-structure}

Permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) revealed
that modelling framework accounted for most of the variation in
multivariate network structure (\(R^2\) = 0.597, \(p\) \textless{}
0.001), whereas temporal turnover across extinction phases explained a
near negligable proportion of variance (\(R^2\) = 0.001, \(p\)
\textless{} 0.001), alongside the model \(\times\) time interaction
(\(R^2\) = 0.03, \(p\) \textless{} 0.001), indicating limited but
significant time-dependent divergence among models. Thus, differences
among models were more than an order of magnitude greater than
differences that could be associated with ecological turnover, despite
the ETEE being characterised with a significant community turnover.

To determine whether the dominance of the model reflected absolute mean
shifts across time, we repeated the analysis after centring network
metrics within each time bin. This procedure removes between-phase
differences while retaining within-phase structural variation. Even
after temporal bin-standardised centring, the model explained the
majority of the observed multivariate variance (\(R^2\) = 0.704, \(p\)
\textless{} 0.001). These results demonstrate that the influence of
modelling approach is not driven by temporal mean differences but
reflects intrinsic divergence among models in how ecological
interactions are organised.

\subsubsection{Statistical Drivers of Network
Variation}\label{statistical-drivers-of-network-variation}

To identify which specific structural properties drive the multivariate
separation observed above, we partitioned variance at the level of
individual network metrics. Results show that model had a significantly
stronger influence on network structure than the ecological signal of
species loss. In panel B of Figure~\ref{fig-structure} for certain
network metrics models predict different responses across time
(\emph{e.g.,} Trophic Index), as well as differing magnitudes of change
(\emph{e.g.,} connectance). A two-way factorial ANOVA across all eight
network metrics confirmed that model was the dominant driver of
variance, with partial eta-squared values (\(\eta^{2}_{p}\)) exceeding
0.7 (Figure~\ref{fig-structure}, panel C; Table S3), except for maximum
trophic level for which \(\eta^{2}_{p} = 0.1\). While time significantly
altered network structure (\(p<0\).001), its relative importance
remained secondary, typically explaining a smaller fraction of the
strucutral variation. This is clear in panel C of
Figure~\ref{fig-structure} where all metrics are within the bottom-right
(model-dominated) triangle (below the dashed line), emphasising that
model assumptions outweigh the ecological signal of extinction-driven
changes. Furthermore, the high inter-model Coefficient of Variation (CV)
observed for some metrics (Table S4, Figure S4) highlights a
sensitivity. The properties that are influenced (albeit modestly) by
time are also those upon which models disagree most profoundly (deeper
colour in panel C of Figure~\ref{fig-structure}). This demonstrates that
our understanding of structural food web collapse in the fossil record
is highly contingent on the chosen model, especially when examining
complex trophic pathways beyond simple macro-scale properties like
connectance.

\subsubsection{Inferred pairwise interactions vary widely among
models}\label{inferred-pairwise-interactions-vary-widely-among-models}

Despite some models showing similar network metrics (\emph{e.g.,} ATN
and ADBM, Figure~\ref{fig-structure}), specific pairwise interactions
often differed. Pairwise \(\beta_{OS}\) revealed that certain model
pairs shared very few links (Figure~\ref{fig-beta_div}). Perhaps most
striking is that the two size-based models (ADBM, ATN) that were broadly
similar in structure still showcase a high degree of interaction
turnover, demonstrating that agreement in overall network structure does
not guarantee agreement in species-level interactions. As the PFWIM is a
metaweb model one would expect that is should have the greatest
dissimilarity with the other body-size constrained models. However,
although there is a high degree of turnover this is not notably higher
than turnover between the body size models. Overall, we see that at the
pairwise level we see that each model tends to have a distinct set of
pairwise interaction from the other. Note that the moderate degree of
within-model interaction turnover can be ascribed to noise during the
network construction process. For the PFIM networks this is due to the
random downsampling, wheres for the body-size constrained models this is
due to to bodysizes being re-simulated for every network.

\subsection{Model choice influences inferred extinction
dynamics}\label{model-choice-influences-inferred-extinction-dynamics}

To evaluate how model choice affects inferred extinction dynamics, we
compared simulated post-extinction networks to observed networks using
mean absolute differences (MAD) for network-level metrics and true
skills statistics (TSS) for node- and link-level outcomes
(Figure~\ref{fig-mad}). Across models, MAD-based rankings were generally
positively correlated (Kendall's \(\tau \approx\) 0.122 across the
structural metrics), indicating weak but generally positive agreement on
the relative importance of extinction mechanisms despite the substantial
differences observed between modelled network structure. The exception
to this is maximum trophic level where we see more disagreement between
the model rankings of extinction mechanisms.

Node-level TSS rankings were similarly consistent across models
(Kendall's \(\tau \approx\) 0.528), reflecting broadly comparable
node-level removal sequences. In contrast, link-level outcomes were far
more variable (Kendall's \(\tau \approx\) 0.143), highlighting that
inferences about which interactions are lost or retained during collapse
and recovery are highly model contingent. However, we see that models
tend to converge on similar species-level extinction patterns, whereas
from link-level extinction processes the inferred pathways of
interaction loss and cascading dynamics depend strongly on the model.

\section{Discussion}\label{discussion}

\subsection{Network reconstruction is not neutral: assumptions shape
ecological
theory}\label{network-reconstruction-is-not-neutral-assumptions-shape-ecological-theory}

Food web reconstruction is often treated as a methodologically neutral
step, with network properties subsequently interpreted as reflections of
underlying ecological organisation. This assumes that reconstructed webs
provide structurally comparable representations of ecological
communities, such that differences in connectance, trophic structure,
motif composition, or robustness reflect biological variation rather
than modelling assumptions.

This assumption is important in deep-time paleoecology, where
interactions are rarely observed directly and must be inferred through
modelling. Although this makes making assumptions unavoidable, it also
renders ecological interpretations sensitive to model choice, creating a
risk that methodological artefacts are misinterpreted as genuine
macroevolutionary or paleoecological signals. The same concern applies
to contemporary food webs, especially as the field increasingly adopts
prediction-forward approaches to network construction (Strydom et al.,
2021).

Our results demonstrate that model choice explains substantially more
variation in food web topology than temporal turnover across extinction
and recovery phases. Despite sharing an identical regional taxon pool,
alternative models generated distinct structural signatures occupying
non-overlapping regions of multivariate space
(Figure~\ref{fig-structure}). Model type remained the dominant driver of
structural variation even after accounting for differences between
phases, indicating that models impose distinct `structural priors' that
shape emergent topology, species roles, and predictions of extinction
dynamics. Network structure is therefore determined not solely by
ecological communities, but jointly by ecological data and the
assumptions used to infer interactions (Gauzens et al., 2025; Strydom et
al., 2026).

Variance partitioning further showed that model choice dominated
variation across individual network properties, suggesting that its
influence extends across multiple dimensions of network organisation
rather than being driven by a single metric. Although this may partly
reflect the propagation of constraints on individual structural
properties into emergent network characteristics (Michalska-Smith \&
Allesina, 2019; Poisot \& Gravel, 2014), constraining connectance to be
identical across all replicates retained structural differences between
models, albeit to a lesser degree (Supplementary Material S2). Notably,
the properties exhibiting the strongest temporal signals also showed the
greatest inter-model disagreement, suggesting that temporal trends are
hardest to resolve where models diverge most strongly (panel C,
Figure~\ref{fig-structure}).

Model disagreement was even more pronounced at the level of individual
pairwise interactions. Models producing structurally similar networks
often inferred substantially different sets of species-level
interactions (Figure~\ref{fig-beta_div}), suggesting that interaction
predictions are sensitive to model choice. This indicates that models
can impose distinct rules governing network structure without agreeing
on the specific links between species pairs. Importantly, this
comparison concerns agreement between predictions, not their accuracy.

This pattern is evident in the ATN and ADBM models. Both showed
substantial variation along LDA axis 2, strongly correlated with the
competition index, but relatively little variation along axis 1,
strongly correlated with the trophic index and vulnerability (Panel A
Figure~\ref{fig-structure}). Together with the limited variation in
trophic index across extinction and recovery phases (panel B,
Figure~\ref{fig-structure}), this suggests that these models constrain
aspects of trophic structure, including the balance of linear chains and
omnivory, despite variation in competitive structure. However, their
structural similarity does not translate into agreement on individual
interactions (Figure~\ref{fig-beta_div}). Thus, models shape not only
the overall structure of inferred food webs but also the ecological
interactions through which that structure is realised.

\subsection{Node- vs link-based
inference}\label{node--vs-link-based-inference}

Importantly, the sensitivity of extinction simulation rankings to
network type was not uniform across levels of inference. Node-level
extinction rankings were broadly consistent among models, whereas
link-level outcomes were model contingent (TSS panel,
Figure~\ref{fig-mad}). This likely reflects the fact that, for
node-specific specifications, primary extinction order is determined by
species traits and is therefore consistent across network types. The
similarity in TSS scores, which evaluate the identity of surviving
species, further suggests that secondary extinctions were also broadly
consistent. However, when primary extinction sequences are determined by
link-level traits, which depend on the inferred network structure, we
see model-specific differences. This suggests that broad species-level
inferences may be robust across plausible interaction architectures, and
that models are broadly assigning species to the same trophic positions.
However, conclusions about interaction loss, retention, and
reorganisation are more depend on how specifc links are assigned to
species.

This distinction challenges a central ambition of food web ecology
related to using interaction structure to identify the mechanisms
underlying stability and collapse. While broad patterns may be robust
across models, mechanistic explanations are far less secure. Had Dunhill
et al. (2024) used a model other than PFWIM, the inferred drivers of
extinction and recovery may have differed substantially. If extinction
mechanisms vary across equally plausible reconstructions, mechanistic
narratives derived from a single inferred topology may overstate their
precision. The apparent determinism of extinction cascades may therefore
reflect model-imposed structure as much as ecological inevitability.

\subsection{Implications for network-based
research}\label{implications-for-network-based-research}

These findings have important implications for comparative network
studies. Because different models encode different ecological
assumptions, observed differences among networks may partly reflect
model choice rather than ecological processes (Brimacombe et al., 2023;
Brimacombe et al., 2024). Without explicit standardisation or
sensitivity analysis, methodological heterogeneity can be mistaken for
biological signal. Food web ecology has devoted substantial effort to
understanding how topology shapes dynamics (Allesina \& Tang, 2012;
Dunne et al., 2002), but comparatively less attention has been paid to
how reconstruction methods shape topology. Our findings indicate that
these questions cannot be separated. However, disagreement among models
does not mean that any single approach is necessarily wrong (Petchey et
al., 2011; Stouffer, 2019). Rather, different frameworks emphasise
different ecological constraints, such as trait compatibility, energetic
optimisation, or topological regularity. The challenge is therefore to
match the model to the question at hand.

As network approaches are increasingly used to investigate ecological
organisation across latitudinal gradients (Tylianakis et al., 2008),
climate-driven changes (Gilman et al., 2010), and stability under
species loss (Hao et al., 2025; Marjakangas et al., 2025), we need to
consider how model choice may influence these results. Studies
frequently interpret variation in connectance, trophic height,
interaction \(\beta\)-diversity, and robustness as evidence of
ecological differentiation across regions or through time (\emph{e.g.,}
Pellissier et al., 2018; Trøjelsgaard \& Olesen, 2016). Our results show
that model choice can systematically alter inferred topology and
disturbance dynamics even when species composition is held constant.
Apparent differences in network structure across spatial or climatic
gradients may therefore reflect model choice as much as underlying
ecological processes. Comparative studies using different modelling
approaches should consequently standardise their methods or explicitly
quantify the sensitivity of their conclusions to alternative network
representations.

No single model is universally correct. Rather, each represents a
distinct hypothesis about how ecological interactions are constrained
(Petchey et al., 2011; Strydom et al., 2026). Model choice should
therefore be treated as part of hypothesis specification, rather than as
a technical step preceding analysis. Researchers must align
network-construction approaches with their ecological questions, whether
these concern potential interactions, realised diets, or broad
structural properties, while recognising that the resulting inferences
may differ in their sensitivity to model choice. Box 1 translates these
findings into practical guidance for selecting network representations
and evaluating the robustness of the conclusions drawn from them.
Recognising this asymmetry is essential for distinguishing robust
ecological patterns from conclusions contingent on modelling
assumptions.

A more robust approach to food web ecology would treat network
construction as a testable component of ecological inference,
incorporating probabilistic link prediction where appropriate and
routinely evaluating the sensitivity of conclusions to alternative
representations of interaction structure. Such an approach aligns with
recent advances in probabilistic and ensemble network modelling
(Banville et al., 2025; Poisot et al., 2016; Wootton et al., 2021). More
fundamentally, it recognises that ecological networks are not simply
representations of communities, but model-dependent representations
through which ecological patterns are identified and interpreted. Their
value as macroecological state variables therefore depends not only on
their capacity to capture ecological organisation, but also on whether
the patterns they reveal persist across plausible representations of
interaction structure.

Network reconstruction is not a neutral step in ecological inference.
Different approaches encode different assumptions about which
interactions are possible, which are likely to occur, and how network
structure is generated. Our results suggest four considerations for
selecting network models and evaluating the conclusions drawn from them.
These are practical guidelines rather than prescriptive rules: the
appropriate approach depends on the ecological question, the available
data, and the level of inference required.

\textbf{1. Define the ecological scale you want to infer}

Begin by specifying whether the objective is to identify potential
interactions, estimate realised trophic links, characterise network
structure, or evaluate ecological dynamics. These objectives are not
interchangeable. A model designed to identify biologically feasible
interactions does not necessarily predict realised diets, while a
structural model can provide a useful reference without reconstructing
the interactions of a specific community. The target of inference should
therefore determine the choice of network representation.

\textbf{2. Match the network representation to the question}

Select a model whose assumptions are appropriate for the ecological
process or property of interest. Trait-based feasibility approaches may
be useful for identifying potential consumer--resource relationships,
while models incorporating energetic, mechanical, or probabilistic
constraints can provide alternative estimates of realised interactions.
Structural models offer theoretical reference points for evaluating
network organisation. However, no model class guarantees accurate
reconstruction, and different approaches may generate distinct network
structures even when applied to the same species pool.

\textbf{3. Test robustness at the level of the conclusion}

Evaluate model sensitivity using the specific quantities that underpin
the ecological claim. Agreement in overall network structure does not
necessarily imply agreement in individual interactions. Where
conclusions depend on the ability to recover pairwise links accurately,
compare link-level predictions directly. Where they concern network
dynamics, repeat the analysis across plausible network representations.
Distinguish conclusions that persist across models from those that
depend on a specific reconstruction. Agreement indicates robustness to
the models considered, not proof of accuracy.

\textbf{4. Distinguish imposed properties from emergent patterns}

Identify which network properties are prescribed by the model, which are
used as inputs or constraints, and which emerge from the resulting
topology. Properties fixed during network construction cannot
subsequently provide independent evidence of ecological differences when
constraints on one property may influence other network metrics. For
example, fixing connectance can constrain the emergent properties of
networks (Poisot \& Gravel, 2014). Interpret these properties in the
context of the assumptions that generated them and consider the intended
application and original purpose of each model.

Together, these considerations encourage researchers to treat network
construction as part of hypothesis specification rather than a technical
step preceding analysis. By aligning model choice with the target of
inference and testing the sensitivity of conclusions to alternative
representations, researchers can better distinguish robust ecological
patterns from those conditional on modelling assumptions.

\end{tcolorbox}

\section{Conclusions}\label{conclusions}

Ecological network reconstruction is not a neutral methodological step;
it shapes ecological inference. By applying six models to the same
species pool, we demonstrate that model choice influences network
structure, interaction identity, and inferred extinction dynamics.
Although broader patterns, such as species retention across extinction
events, were relatively robust, fine-scale interactions varied
considerably among models, highlighting that robustness at one level of
ecological organisation does not necessarily extend to others. This has
implications beyond paleoecology, as differences in network properties
between communities may reflect reconstruction assumptions rather than
ecological processes alone. Reliable macroecological inference therefore
requires comparing alternative network representations, selecting models
suited to specific research objectives, and evaluating sensitivity at
the level of ecological conclusions. Such comparisons help distinguish
robust patterns from those contingent on modelling assumptions.
Ultimately, the value of ecological networks lies not simply in
reconstructing ecological structure, but in establishing which
inferences remain reliable across plausible representations.
