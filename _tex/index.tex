  \begin{abstract}
    \textbf{1.} Ecological networks are increasingly used to compare
    community organisation, quantify interaction turnover, and infer
    responses to environmental change. However, interactions are often
    incompletely observed and must be reconstructed using models that
    encode different ecological assumptions. The extent to which these
    choices shape ecological inference remains underexplored.

    \textbf{2.} We examine this problem by reconstructing four
    successive marine assemblages spanning the Early Toarcian extinction
    and recovery in the Cleveland Basin, UK. We compare six food-web
    models spanning feasibility-based, realised, and structural
    representations, holding species composition constant within each
    assemblage. We quantify network topology, pairwise interaction
    turnover, and inferred extinction dynamics.

    \textbf{3.} Model choice explained substantially more variation in
    network structure than temporal turnover across extinction and
    recovery phases. Models producing similar network-level properties
    nevertheless inferred substantially different species-level
    interactions. Extinction scenario rankings were broadly consistent
    at the species level, but interaction-level outcomes and cascade
    dynamics were highly model contingent.

    \textbf{4.} These findings demonstrate that the reliability of
    network-based inference depends on the scale of the ecological
    question. Similar network structures do not guarantee agreement on
    individual interactions, and apparently robust aggregate patterns
    may coexist with substantial uncertainty in inferred mechanisms.
    Network reconstruction should therefore be treated as an explicit
    component of hypothesis specification, with approaches matched to
    inferential goals and conclusions tested across alternative network
    representations.

      \end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

Understanding how biological communities are organised and how species
interact with each other is a central goal of ecology. While early
efforts focused primarily on species richness and composition, there is
growing recognition that ecological communities are structured not only
by which species occur, but by how they interact (Gravel \emph{et al.},
2019; Thuiller \emph{et al.}, 2024; Mestre \& Bastazini, 2026).
Interaction networks are increasingly treated as macroecological state
variables where they are used to compare community organisation across
environmental gradients (Tylianakis \& Morris, 2017), quantify
\(\beta\)-diversity in interaction structure (Poisot \emph{et al.},
2015; Trøjelsgaard \& Olesen, 2016), evaluate stability--complexity
relationships (Allesina \& Tang, 2012), and infer vulnerability under
global change (Hao \emph{et al.}, 2025).

As a result, ecological networks now play a central role in comparative
analyses spanning latitudinal gradients, disturbance regimes, and deep
time environmental transitions (Roopnarine, 2006; Poisot \& Gravel,
2014; Michalska-Smith \& Allesina, 2019; Dunhill \emph{et al.}, 2024;
Hao \emph{et al.}, 2025). Implicit in this expansion is the critical
assumption that network properties estimated across systems using
various models are structurally comparable, and that differences among
network properties reflect ecological signal rather than methodological
artefact (Fründ \emph{et al.}, 2016; Jordano, 2016). However, most
ecological networks are not fully observed as interaction data are
incomplete and sampling is uneven across historical and biogeographic
contexts, across both present day and deep time (Poisot \emph{et al.},
2021; Sandra \emph{et al.}, 2025).

Interactions must often be inferred indirectly from traits (\emph{e.g.,}
body size), phylogeny, co-occurrence, or theoretical constraints
(Morales-Castilla \emph{et al.}, 2015; Strydom \emph{et al.}, 2021a).
Network construction therefore constitutes a model-based inference step
rather than a purely observational exercise. Different network
construction frameworks encode distinct ecological assumptions about how
interactions arise - whether as biologically feasible combinations of
traits, energetically optimised realised diets, or topological
structures. Note for the purpose of this work we will use the term
`model' to encompass the different approaches used to construct
networks, acknowledging that the different frameworks are themselves
different `models'. These assumptions act as structural priors over
network architecture (Petchey \emph{et al.}, 2011; Guimarães, 2020;
Gauzens \emph{et al.}, 2025; Strydom \emph{et al.}, 2026) and begs the
question if alternative approaches systematically generate different
trophic configurations. Risking conflating ecological differences among
communities with artefacts introduced by modelling choice. The
reliability of macroecological inference therefore depends not only on
ecological data, but on the structural assumptions embedded in the
modelling approach.

Despite rapid methodological development in interaction inference, few
studies have directly evaluated how alternative approaches to
constructing networks influence macroecological conclusions when applied
to the same species pool. This gap is particularly consequential for
comparative research, where network metrics are routinely interpreted as
indicators of environmental filtering, disturbance intensity,
evolutionary history, or community stability (Allesina \& Tang, 2012;
Poisot \emph{et al.}, 2015; Delmas \emph{et al.}, 2018) and begs the
question if models encode distinct structural constraints over
interaction topology, then differences among communities may reflect
model assumptions rather than ecological processes.

Deep time ecosystems provide an especially useful test of this issue
because trophic interactions are not observed directly and must be
inferred from traits, co-occurrence, and theoretical constraints
(Roopnarine, 2006; Dunne \emph{et al.}, 2008; Dunne \emph{et al.}, 2014;
Dunhill \emph{et al.}, 2024; Smith \emph{et al.}, 2025). As a result,
ecological networks in these systems are inherently model-dependent,
rendering the assumptions embedded within different models both explicit
and consequential. Among such systems, the Early Toarcian Extinction
Event (ETEE; \textasciitilde183 Ma) provides a particularly informative
case study. The ETEE was a major Early Jurassic biotic crisis associated
with rapid climatic warming, widespread marine oxygen depletion, and
substantial ecological turnover (Kemp \emph{et al.}, 2024). Although
considered a second-order extinction event globally, it nevertheless
resulted in the loss of approximately 26\% of marine genera worldwide
(Little \& Benton, 1995). Ecological disruption was especially severe in
shallow marine ecosystems, where environmental stress drove extensive
restructuring of benthic communities and food webs. In the Cleveland
Basin of Yorkshire, the ETEE caused the extinction of around 60\% of
marine species, including up to 87\% of benthic taxa (Caswell \emph{et
al.}, 2009). Fossil assemblages from this interval therefore record both
the collapse of established communities and the subsequent recovery and
reassembly of marine ecosystems under rapidly changing environmental
conditions. Recent work has shown that the event selectively impacted
specialist and infaunal organisms, leading to simplified ecosystems
increasingly dominated by ecological generalists and opportunistic taxa
(Dunhill \emph{et al.}, 2024). These changes were accompanied by major
shifts in community structure, trophic interactions, and ecosystem
connectivity, with evidence suggesting that ecological recovery
following the extinction was both prolonged and uneven across marine
habitats.

Ecological networks have previously been used to infer patterns of
community organisation and extinction dynamics across this transition,
providing an ideal test case for evaluating the robustness of
network-based inferences of community response. Recent work by Dunhill
\emph{et al.} (2024) has used reconstructed food webs to draw ecological
conclusions about community stability and collapse during this event.
This creates a rare opportunity to ask a more general question - to what
extent are such inferences contingent on the specific model used to
generate the network?

Here, we reconstruct ecological networks for four successive assemblages
across the ETEE using six contrasting modelling frameworks spanning
feasibility-based, realised, and structural models. Holding species
composition constant while varying only the model allows us to
explicitly evaluate whether conclusions drawn from a single model (such
as those of Dunhill \emph{et al.} (2024)) are robust to alternative,
equally plausible representations of interaction structure, or whether
different models would lead to fundamentally different ecological
inferences. More broadly, our study tests whether macroecological
conclusions derived from ecological networks reflect underlying
biological processes or are conditioned by the assumptions embedded
within the modelling framework itself.

\section{Methods}\label{methods}

\subsection{Study system and fossil
data}\label{study-system-and-fossil-data}

We used fossil occurrence data from the Cleveland Basin, UK, spanning
the late Pliensbachian to the late Toarcian. This interval encompasses a
major volcanic-driven hyperthermal and marine extinction event. To
capture network dynamics across this transition, we defined four
successive palaeo-assemblages: pre-extinction (Pliensbachian),
post-extinction (early Toarcian), early recovery (early--middle
Toarcian) and late recovery (late Toarcian). Each taxon was
characterised using their size and Bambach's ecospace framework (Bambach
\emph{et al.}, 2007), coding for tiering, motility, and feeding mode as
per Dunhill \emph{et al.} (2024). Each assemblage was treated as a
community of potentially interacting taxa. The dataset includes 57 taxa
across diverse groups (\emph{e.g.,} cephalopods, bivalves, and
gastropods). See Dunhill \emph{et al.} (2024) for detailed descriptions
of the dataset, including taxonomic composition, trait coding, and
temporal resolution.

\subsection{Network construction
models}\label{network-construction-models}

\subsubsection{Conceptual classification of network
types}\label{conceptual-classification-of-network-types}

Ecological network reconstruction encompasses a range of approaches that
differ in how trophic interactions are inferred and in the ecological
assumptions they encode. These approaches can be broadly grouped into
three classes: feasibility-based, realised, and structural models.
Together, these classes represent distinct hypotheses about how
interactions arise and constrain the space of possible food web
structure (Strydom \emph{et al.}, 2026).

Feasibility-based models infer the set of potential interactions among
species based on trait compatibility or mechanistic rules. These
approaches define a feasible interaction space by identifying which
consumer--resource pairs are biologically possible, without specifying
which interactions are realised in each community. In palaeoecological
contexts, where direct observation of interactions is not possible, such
models provide a biologically grounded, formalised representation, of
expert knowledge based rules that can be used to infer interactions
(\emph{e.g.,} Fricke \emph{et al.} (2022); Roopnarine (2006); Shaw
\emph{et al.} (2024)).

Realised models aim to approximate the subset of interactions that are
expected to occur in practice. These approaches impose additional
constraints (such as energetic optimisation, mechanical limits, or
probabilistic niche structure) on top of feasibility, thereby generating
networks that represent putative realised diets (Brose \emph{et al.},
2006; Schneider \emph{et al.}, 2016). Although often parameterised using
body size or related traits, realised models differ in their underlying
ecological assumptions about how consumers select resources and how
trophic interactions are structured.

Structural models, in contrast, do not attempt to reconstruct empirical
trophic interactions from species-level data. Instead, they generate
networks based on general topological constraints such as species
richness, connectance, or trophic ordering (Allesina \emph{et al.},
2008). These models are species-agnostic and therefore cannot be
interpreted as reconstructions of specific ecological communities.
Rather, they have the potential to serve as `null hypotheses', providing
reference expectations against which the structure and dynamics of
data-informed reconstructions can be evaluated.

In this study, we selected models to span these three conceptual classes
(Table~\ref{tbl-models}) to sample distinct mechanistic interpretations
of trophic interactions rather than exhaustively include all available
implementations. Specifically, we include: a feasibility-based model
(PFWIM), representing trait-constrained interactions; multiple realised
models (ADBM, ATN, and Body-size ratio), capturing energetic
optimisation, mechanical constraints, and probabilistic allometric
structure, respectively; and structural models (Random (Erdős-Rényi
model) and Niche), providing theoretical reference points. Notably some
approaches share common inputs (\emph{e.g.,} body mass for ADBM, ATN,
and Body-size ratio), they encode fundamentally different assumptions
about how interactions arise. Additionally, although other
feasibility-based approaches exist (\emph{e.g.,} Roopnarine (2006)),
they are conceptually similar to PFWIM in that they infer interactions
from biologically feasible consumer--resource combinations. We therefore
selected PFWIM as a representative feasibility-based framework because
it formalises these rules in a transferable model that can be
parameterised across datasets, rather than relying on system-specific
expert assignment of links.

This classification allows us to distinguish variation arising from
ecological assumptions embedded in models from variation attributable to
species composition. By comparing networks generated from the same
species pool across these complementary model classes, we explicitly
evaluate how different representations of interaction structure
condition ecological inference.

\subsubsection{Network generation and
replication}\label{network-generation-and-replication}

We evaluated six network reconstruction models using the approaches
listed in Table~\ref{tbl-models}, expanded descriptions of model
assumptions, parameterisation, and link-generation rules are provided in
Supplementary Material S1, including full mathematical formulations and
parameter definitions. For each community, 100 stochastic network
realisations were generated per model (n = 2400 in total, 600 per time
bin). Of the simulated networks we excluded those that had a network
metric that was considered an outlier (\(> 3\sigma\) from the mean).
This resulted in the removal of 13 networks (of which 3 were ATN model
networks and the rest Random model networks), typically due to very
large trophic level values. Where models required species body mass or
trait values, these were uniformly sampled within the different size
classes. This was done by sampling from a truncated lognormal
distribution, with the the upper and lower bounds of each size class
serving as the boundaries. Alternative distributions (lognormal and
uniform) were tested, and shown to have a minimal impact on topology
(Supplementary Material S1; Figure S1). Random and Niche models were
parameterised using connectance values drawn from an empirically
realistic range (0.05--0.25; Curtsdotter \emph{et al.} (2011)). For the
Random model, which requires link number explicitly, connectance (Co)
was first converted to expected link number (L) using
\(L = Co\times S^2\), ensuring consistency in network density across all
models. Richness (S) was determined by the number of species for each
time bin. For the Body-size ratio model, we followed the approach of
Yeakel \emph{et al.} (2014) using only the body-mass scaling to
determine links between species. Sensitivity of network structure to
these parameter choices is evaluated in Supplementary Material S1. The
PFWIM generates a deterministic feasible metaweb for a given trait
configuration. To allow for statistical analyses we introduced variation
by applying a downsampling procedure that randomly pruned links until
the connectance of the network decreased by 0.1. This step allowed us to
introduce statistical variation while still preserving most of the
original metaweb structure. The choice of the magnitude of connectance
change and its effects on network structure are discussed in
Supplementary Material S1.

\subsection{Network metrics and structural
analyses}\label{network-metrics-and-structural-analyses}

We quantified network structure using a suite of network metrics
Table~\ref{tbl-properties}, capturing overall network properties, motif
structure, and species-level variability. Differences among models were
assessed using a multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA), with model
identity as a fixed factor and the full set of network metrics as
response variables. Variance partitioning was further assessed using
permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA). Pairwise
interaction turnover was quantified using link-based \(\beta\)-diversity
and was calculated following the framework of Poisot \emph{et al.}
(2012) among the four of the modelling frameworks (ADBM, ATN, body-size
ratio, and PFWIM). Random and Niche models were excluded because they
are species-agnostic models that generate networks matching broad
topological properties (\emph{e.g.,} connectance and degree
distributions) rather than predicting biologically realistic
species-specific interactions. Specifically, we looked at interaction
rewiring among shared species (\(\beta_{OS}\), see S1 for additional
details on the decomposition of interaction β-diversity and its
implementation), which allows separation of differences arising from
altered interaction identities among species common to both networks.
Because all networks within a given assemblage are constructed from the
same species pool, differences in interaction structure primarily
reflect `rewiring' of trophic links due to stochasticity in the model
rather than species turnover. All calculations were performed for all
model combinations within the same assemblage (time bin).

\subsection{Extinction simulations and their
evaluation}\label{extinction-simulations-and-their-evaluation}

Following Dunhill \emph{et al.} (2024), we simulated species loss from
pre-extinction networks under trait-based, network-position--based, and
random removal scenarios. Species were deleted sequentially, with
cascading secondary extinctions allowed to propagate. Simulated
post-extinction states were compared to observed (\emph{i.e.,}
reconstructed from fossil occurrence data) networks using mean absolute
differences (MAD) of food web metrics (Table~\ref{tbl-properties}) and
modified true skill statistics (TSS) calculated separately at the node
level (species presence/absence) and link level (presence/absence of
interactions between species pairs). Scenarios were ranked within each
model based on MAD and TSS performance, and Kendall's rank correlation
coefficient (\(\tau\)) was used to quantify agreement in scenario
ordering across models. Detailed implementation of extinction sequences
and secondary extinction rules is provided in Supplementary Material S1.

\subsection{Software and
Reproducibility}\label{software-and-reproducibility}

Ecological network reconstruction and extraction of structural metrics
were conducted in Julia v1.11.4 (Bezanson \emph{et al.}, 2017) using the
model implementations provided in Supplementary Material S1. All
statistical analyses, including MANOVA, PERMANOVA, and post hoc
comparisons, were performed in R v4.5.2 (R Core Team, 2024). The full
analytical workflow, including data preprocessing, network generation,
extinction simulations, and metric calculation, is fully reproducible
from the archived codebase.

\section{Results}\label{results}

We found that different models (even those that appeared structurally
similar) yielded fundamentally different ecological inferences. Across
the six models the inferred food web structure, species interactions,
and extinction dynamics differed. Multivariate analyses showed
pronounced separation among models in network structure space
(Figure~\ref{fig-structure}), with model explaining most of the variance
in structural properties (Figure~\ref{fig-anova}). Notably, we had a
consistenly high rate of species turnover between models
(Figure~\ref{fig-beta_div}) and differnces in inferences around
extinction dynamics (Figure~\ref{fig-mad}), demonstrating that
structural similarity does not guarantee similarity in species-level
diets or inferred behaviour of the system.

Model substantially influenced inferred extinction dynamics. Temporal
trajectories of network collapse, interaction loss, and motif
reorganisation differed among approaches (Figure~\ref{fig-anova}).
Although node-level extinction rankings were often broadly consistent,
link-level outcomes and extinction inferences were highly sensitive to
model assumptions (Figure~\ref{fig-mad}). Together, these results show
that ecological inferences drawn from networks are dependant on the
model used.

\subsection{Network structure differs among
models}\label{network-structure-differs-among-models}

Across modelling approache, network structure (network properties listed
in Table~\ref{tbl-properties}) differed significantly (MANOVA, Pillai's
trace = 2.79, approximate \(F_{30,11880}\) = 502.1, \(p<\) 0.001),
indicating that the model choice systematically alters inferred food web
topology. Linear discriminant analysis (LDA) identified two dominant
axes of variation, explaining 95.2\% of the differences between models.
LDA axis one correlated with trophic index, vulnerability, and
generality. LDA axis two correlated with competition index, generality,
and connectance (Figure~\ref{fig-structure}; Table S1, Figure S1).
Broadly indicating that structural differences are driven by the way in
which the models assign links to species as opposed to macro-level
network properties such as maximum trophic level.

\subsubsection{Variance partitioning of network
structure}\label{variance-partitioning-of-network-structure}

Permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) revealed
that modelling framework accounted for most of the variation in
multivariate network structure (\(R^2\) = 0.597, \(p\) \textless{}
0.001), whereas temporal turnover across extinction phases explained a
near negligable proportion of variance (\(R^2\) = 0.001, \(p\)
\textless{} 0.001), alongside the model \(\times\) time interaction
(\(R^2\) = 0.03, \(p\) \textless{} 0.001), indicating limited but
significant time-dependent divergence among models. Thus, differences
among models were more than an order of magnitude greater than
differences that could be associated with ecological turnover through
the extinction-recovery period, even though the Pliensbachian--Toarcian
dataset was characterised with a significant community turnover.

To determine whether the dominance of the model reflected absolute mean
shifts among time bins, we repeated the analysis after centring network
metrics within each extinction phase. This procedure removes
between-phase differences while retaining within-phase structural
variation. Even after temporal bin-standardised centring, the model
explained the majarity of the observed multivariate variance (\(R^2\) =
0.704, \(p\) \textless{} 0.001). These results demonstrate that the
influence of modelling approach is not driven by temporal mean
differences but reflects intrinsic divergence among models in how
ecological interactions are organised.

\subsubsection{Statistical Drivers of Network
Variation}\label{statistical-drivers-of-network-variation}

To identify which specific structural properties drive the multivariate
separation observed above, we partitioned variance at the level of
individual network metrics. Results show that model had a significantly
stronger influence on network structure than the ecological signal of
species loss. In panel A of Figure~\ref{fig-anova} for certain network
metrics models predict different responses across time (\emph{e.g.,}
Trophic Index), as well as differing magnitudes of change (\emph{e.g.,}
connectance). A two-way factorial ANOVA across all eight network metrics
confirmed that model was the dominant driver of variance, with partial
eta-squared values (\(\eta^{2}_{p}\)) exceeding 0.7
(Figure~\ref{fig-anova}, panel B; Table S3), with the exception of
maximum trophic level for which \(\eta^{2}_{p} = 0.1\). While time (pre
to post extinction recovery) significantly altered network structure
(\(p<0\).001), its relative importance remained secondary, typically
explaining a smaller fraction of the strucutral variation. This is clear
in panel B of Figure~\ref{fig-anova} where all metrics are within the
bottom-right (model-dominated) triangle (below the dashed line),
emphasising that model assumptions outweigh the ecological signal of
extinction-driven changes. Furthermore, the high inter-model Coefficient
of Variation (CV) observed for some metrics (Table S4, Figure S4)
highlights a sensitivity. The properties that are influenced (albeit
modestly) by time are also those upon which models disagree most
profoundly (larger point size). This demonstrates that our understanding
of structural food web collapse in the fossil record is highly
contingent on the chosen model, particularly when examining complex
trophic pathways beyond simple macro-scale properties like connectance.

\subsection{Model choice influences inferred extinction
dynamics}\label{model-choice-influences-inferred-extinction-dynamics}

To evaluate how model choice affects inferred extinction dynamics, we
compared simulated post-extinction networks to observed networks using
mean absolute differences (MAD) for network-level metrics and true
skills statistics (TSS) for node- and link-level outcomes
(Figure~\ref{fig-mad}). Across models, MAD-based rankings were generally
positively correlated (Kendall's \(\tau\) ≈ 0.122 across the structural
metrics), indicating weak but generally positive agreement on the
relative importance of extinction mechanisms despite the substantial
differences observed between modelled network structure. The exception
to this is maximum trophic level where we see more disagreement between
the model rankings of extinction mechanisms.

Node-level TSS rankings were similarly consistent across models
(Kendall's \(\tau\) ≈ 0.528), reflecting broadly comparable node-level
removal sequences. In contrast, link-level outcomes were far more
variable (Kendall's \(\tau\) ≈ 0.143), highlighting that inferences
about which interactions are lost or retained during collapse and
recovery are highly model contingent. However we see that models tend to
converge on similar species-level extinction patterns, whereas from
link-level extinction processes the inferred pathways of interaction
loss and cascading dynamics depend strongly on the model.

\section{Discussion}\label{discussion}

\subsection{Network reconstruction is not neutral: assumptions shape
ecological
theory}\label{network-reconstruction-is-not-neutral-assumptions-shape-ecological-theory}

Food web reconstruction is often treated as a methodologically neutral
step, with network properties subsequently interpreted as reflections of
underlying ecological organisation. This assumes that reconstructed webs
provide structurally comparable representations of ecological
communities, such that differences in connectance, trophic structure,
motif composition, or robustness reflect biological variation rather
than modelling assumptions.

This assumption is particularly important in deep-time palaeoecology,
where interactions are rarely observed directly and must be inferred
through modelling. Although this makes making assumptions unavoidable,
it also renders ecological interpretations sensitive to model choice,
creating a risk that methodological artefacts are misinterpreted as
genuine macroevolutionary or palaeoecological signals. The same concern
applies to contemporary food webs, particularly as the field
increasingly adopts prediction-forward approaches to network
construction (Strydom \emph{et al.}, 2021b).

Our results demonstrate that model choice explains substantially more
variation in food web topology than temporal turnover across extinction
and recovery phases. Despite sharing an identical regional taxon pool,
alternative models generated distinct structural signatures occupying
non-overlapping regions of multivariate space
(Figure~\ref{fig-structure}). Model type remained the dominant driver of
structural variation even after accounting for differences between
phases, indicating that models impose distinct \emph{structural priors}
that shape emergent topology, species roles, and predictions of
extinction dynamics. Network structure is therefore determined not
solely by ecological communities, but jointly by ecological data and the
assumptions used to infer interactions (Gauzens \emph{et al.}, 2025;
Strydom \emph{et al.}, 2026).

Variance partitioning further showed that model choice dominated
variation across individual network properties, suggesting that its
influence extends across multiple dimensions of network organisation
rather than being driven by a single metric. Although this may partly
reflect the propagation of constraints on individual structural
properties into emergent network characteristics (Poisot \& Gravel,
2014; Michalska-Smith \& Allesina, 2019), constraining connectance to be
identical across all replicates retained structural differences between
models, albeit to a lesser degree (Supplementary Material S2). Notably,
the properties exhibiting the strongest temporal signals also showed the
greatest inter-model disagreement, suggesting that temporal trends are
hardest to resolve where models diverge most strongly (panel B,
Figure~\ref{fig-anova}).

Model disagreement was even more pronounced at the level of individual
pairwise interactions. Models producing structurally similar networks
often inferred substantially different sets of species-level
interactions (Figure~\ref{fig-beta_div}), suggesting that interaction
predictions are particularly sensitive to model choice. This indicates
that models can impose distinct rules governing network structure
without agreeing on the specific links between species pairs.
Importantly, this comparison concerns agreement between predictions, not
their accuracy.

This pattern is particularly evident in the ATN and ADBM models. Both
showed substantial variation along LDA axis 2, strongly correlated with
the competition index, but relatively little variation along axis 1,
strongly correlated with the trophic index and vulnerability
(Figure~\ref{fig-structure}). Together with the limited variation in
trophic index across extinction and recovery phases (panel A,
Figure~\ref{fig-anova}), this suggests that these models constrain
aspects of trophic structure, including the balance of linear chains and
omnivory, despite variation in competitive structure. However, their
structural similarity does not translate into agreement on individual
interactions (Figure~\ref{fig-beta_div}). Thus, models shape not only
the overall structure of inferred food webs but also the ecological
interactions through which that structure is realised.

\subsection{Node- vs link-based
inference}\label{node--vs-link-based-inference}

Importantly, the sensitivity of extinction simulation rankings to
network type was not uniform across levels of inference. Node-level
extinction rankings were broadly consistent among models, whereas
link-level outcomes were model contingent (TSS panel,
Figure~\ref{fig-mad}). This likely reflects the fact that, for
node-specific specifications, primary extinction order is determined by
species traits and is therefore consistent across network types. The
similarity in TSS scores, which evaluate the identity of surviving
species, further suggests that secondary extinctions were also broadly
consistent. However, when primary extinction sequences are determined by
link-level traits, which depend on the inferred network structure, we
see model-specific differences. This suggests that broad species-level
inferences may be robust across plausible interaction architectures,
whereas conclusions about interaction loss, retention, and
reorganisation depend more strongly on how interactions are inferred.

This distinction challenges a central ambition of food web ecology:
using interaction structure to identify the mechanisms underlying
stability and collapse. While broad patterns may be robust across
models, mechanistic explanations are far less secure. Had Dunhill
\emph{et al.} (2024) used a model other than PFWIM, the inferred drivers
of extinction and recovery may have differed substantially. If
extinction mechanisms vary across equally plausible reconstructions,
mechanistic narratives derived from a single inferred topology may
overstate their precision. The apparent determinism of extinction
cascades may therefore reflect model-imposed structure as much as
ecological inevitability.

\subsection{Implications for network-based
research}\label{implications-for-network-based-research}

These findings have important implications for comparative network
studies. Because different models encode different ecological
assumptions, observed differences among networks may partly reflect
model choice rather than ecological processes (Brimacombe \emph{et al.},
2023, 2024). Without explicit standardisation or sensitivity analysis,
methodological heterogeneity can be mistaken for biological signal. Food
web ecology has devoted substantial effort to understanding how topology
shapes dynamics (Dunne \emph{et al.}, 2002; Allesina \& Tang, 2012), but
comparatively less attention has been paid to how reconstruction methods
shape topology. Our findings indicate that these questions cannot be
separated. However, disagreement among models does not mean that any
single approach is necessarily wrong (Petchey \emph{et al.}, 2011;
Stouffer, 2019). Rather, different frameworks emphasise different
ecological constraints, such as trait compatibility, energetic
optimisation, or topological regularity. The challenge is therefore to
match the model to the question at hand.

As network approaches are increasingly used to investigate ecological
organisation across latitudinal gradients (Tylianakis \emph{et al.},
2008), climate-driven changes (Gilman \emph{et al.}, 2010), and
stability under species loss (Hao \emph{et al.}, 2025; Marjakangas
\emph{et al.}, 2025), we need to consider how model choice may influence
these results. Studies frequently interpret variation in connectance,
trophic height, interaction (\beta)-diversity, and robustness as
evidence of ecological differentiation across regions or through time
(e.g., Pellissier \emph{et al.}, 2018; Trøjelsgaard \& Olesen, 2016).
Our results show that model choice can systematically alter inferred
topology and disturbance dynamics even when species composition is held
constant. Apparent differences in network structure across spatial or
climatic gradients may therefore reflect model choice as much as
underlying ecological processes. Comparative studies using different
modelling appraoches should consequently standardise their methods or
explicitly quantify the sensitivity of their conclusions to alternative
network representations.

No single model is universally correct. Rather, each represents a
distinct hypothesis about how ecological interactions are constrained
(Petchey \emph{et al.}, 2011; Strydom \emph{et al.}, 2026). Modelling
choice should therefore be viewed as part of hypothesis specification
rather than a technical step preceding analysis. Researchers must align
modelling approaches with their ecological questions, whether estimating
potential interactions, realised diets, or broad structural properties,
because each framework emphasises different aspects of ecological
organisation. Our results show that the consequences of these choices
depend on the scale of inference. Some conclusions, such as those
related to species traits proved to be more robust to model choice than
those related to node-level properties. Network-based inference may
therefore be robust at coarse ecological scales while remaining
imprecise in its mechanistic detail. Recognising this asymmetry is
essential for distinguishing robust ecological patterns from
model-dependent artefacts.

A more mature modelling paradigm in food web ecology would treat network
construction as a testable hypotheses, incorporate probabilistic link
inference where possible, and routinely evaluate the sensitivity of
ecological conclusions to alternative representations of interaction
structure. Such an approach aligns with recent advances in probabilistic
and ensemble network modelling and would strengthen the interpretability
of network-based inference under global change (Poisot \emph{et al.},
2016; Banville \emph{et al.}, 2025).

\section{Conclusions}\label{conclusions}

Ecological network reconstruction is not a neutral methodological step,
but a theoretical choice that shapes ecological inference. Applying six
contrasting models to an identical species pool, we show that model
choice systematically influences inferred network structure, interaction
identity, and disturbance dynamics. While broad patterns, such as
relative species vulnerability, were comparatively robust, fine-scale
interactions and extinction cascades were highly model contingent. The
reliability of network-based inference is therefore scale dependent.

These findings challenge the assumption that reconstructed networks are
directly comparable across systems and through time. Differences in
network structure and dynamics may reflect model assumptions as much as
underlying ecological processes. Reconstruction should therefore be
treated as an explicit component of hypothesis specification, rather
than a technical step preceding analysis.

No single model captures the full complexity of ecological organisation,
nor are alternative approaches interchangeable. Matching models to
inferential goals, standardising approaches across comparative studies,
and testing conclusions across alternative network representations are
essential to distinguish robust ecological patterns from model-dependent
outcomes.
